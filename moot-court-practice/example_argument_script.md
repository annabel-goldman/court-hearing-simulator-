# Example Moot Court Argument Script

This is a sample argument you can read during practice sessions. The script includes natural pause points where a judge might interrupt with questions.

---

## Sample Argument: First Amendment Challenge

**Case Context:**
- **Case Name:** Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission
- **Your Role:** Petitioner (arguing against campaign finance restrictions)
- **Legal Issues:** First Amendment, corporate speech, campaign finance regulation

---

### Opening Statement

"May it please the Court. My name is [Your Name], and I represent the petitioner in this case.

Today, I will address three key points. First, the regulation at issue violates the First Amendment's protection of political speech. Second, the government's asserted interest in preventing corruption does not justify this broad restriction. And third, the regulation is unconstitutionally vague and overbroad.

I would like to begin with the First Amendment issue."

---

### First Point: First Amendment Violation

"The First Amendment protects political speech, and there can be no question that the speech at issue here is political in nature. The documentary film in question addresses matters of public concern, specifically the qualifications and positions of a candidate for federal office.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that political speech lies at the heart of the First Amendment's protection. In Buckley v. Valeo, the Court recognized that discussion of public issues and debate on the qualifications of candidates are integral to the operation of our system of government.

Here, the government seeks to restrict this core political speech based solely on the identity of the speaker—a corporation. But the identity of the speaker should not determine the level of constitutional protection afforded to speech."

**[PAUSE - Judge might ask about the distinction between corporate and individual speech]**

"The Court has previously recognized that corporations have First Amendment rights. In First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, this Court held that the First Amendment does not allow distinctions to be drawn based on the identity of the speaker. The speech itself, not the speaker, is what matters for First Amendment purposes.

The government's argument that corporate speech should receive less protection is fundamentally at odds with this principle. A corporation's ability to speak on matters of public concern should be protected to the same extent as an individual's speech."

**[PAUSE - Judge might ask about the limits of this principle or about different types of corporations]**

---

### Second Point: Government Interest

"Turning to the government's asserted interest, the government claims that this regulation is necessary to prevent corruption or the appearance of corruption. However, the evidence does not support this claim.

First, the regulation is not narrowly tailored to address corruption. It sweeps far too broadly, restricting speech that has no connection to corruption whatsoever. The documentary film at issue here is not a campaign contribution. It is independent political speech that does not create any quid pro quo arrangement or appearance of corruption.

Second, the government has failed to demonstrate that the type of speech restricted here actually leads to corruption. The Court has previously recognized that independent expenditures, as opposed to direct contributions, do not pose the same risk of corruption."

**[PAUSE - Judge might ask about the distinction between expenditures and contributions, or about evidence of corruption]**

"The distinction between contributions and expenditures is crucial here. When a corporation makes an independent expenditure, there is no direct connection between the corporation and the candidate. The candidate does not receive the money, and there is no opportunity for a quid pro quo arrangement.

In contrast, direct contributions create a direct financial relationship between the contributor and the candidate, which could potentially lead to corruption. But that is not what we have here. This is an independent expenditure, and the government has not shown that independent expenditures by corporations lead to corruption."

**[PAUSE - Judge might ask about the appearance of corruption or about specific examples]**

---

### Third Point: Vagueness and Overbreadth

"Finally, even if the government had a compelling interest, the regulation is unconstitutionally vague and overbroad. The regulation fails to provide clear guidance about what speech is prohibited and what speech is permitted.

The term 'electioneering communication' is defined so broadly that it could encompass a wide range of speech that has nothing to do with elections. A documentary film, a book, or even a news article could potentially fall within this definition if it mentions a candidate's name within a certain time period before an election.

This vagueness creates a chilling effect on speech. Speakers cannot know with certainty whether their speech will be subject to regulation, and this uncertainty causes them to self-censor rather than risk violating the law."

**[PAUSE - Judge might ask about the specific language of the regulation or about the chilling effect]**

"The overbreadth of the regulation is equally problematic. The regulation restricts far more speech than is necessary to achieve the government's stated interest. It applies to all corporations, regardless of their size, purpose, or connection to the election.

A small nonprofit corporation that produces a documentary film receives the same treatment as a large for-profit corporation that makes direct campaign contributions. This one-size-fits-all approach cannot survive constitutional scrutiny."

**[PAUSE - Judge might ask about whether the regulation could be narrowed or about severability]**

---

### Rebuttal Preparation

"If the government argues that this regulation is necessary to prevent corporate influence over elections, I would respond that the First Amendment does not permit the government to restrict speech based on concerns about the speaker's influence. The solution to speech we don't like is more speech, not less.

If the government argues that corporations have special advantages that justify different treatment, I would respond that the First Amendment does not permit the government to level the playing field by restricting speech. The government cannot restrict some speakers to enhance the relative voice of others."

**[PAUSE - Judge might ask about the practical implications or about alternative solutions]**

---

### Conclusion

"In conclusion, the regulation at issue violates the First Amendment in multiple ways. It restricts core political speech, it is not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest, and it is unconstitutionally vague and overbroad.

The Court should strike down this regulation and affirm that political speech, regardless of the speaker's identity, is entitled to the highest level of constitutional protection.

Thank you, Your Honor."

## Tips for Using This Script

1. **Read naturally** - Don't rush. Speak at a normal pace as if you're actually in court.

2. **Pause at marked points** - The **[PAUSE]** markers indicate good places where a judge might naturally interrupt. You can pause briefly, but don't wait too long—just continue if there's no interruption.

3. **Be ready for questions** - When the judge interrupts:
   - Listen to the question carefully
   - Take a moment to think
   - Answer directly and concisely
   - Then continue with your argument

4. **Adapt as needed** - Feel free to modify this script or create your own based on your actual case.

5. **Practice different sections** - You don't need to read the entire script each time. Practice individual sections to work on specific skills.

## Alternative: Shorter Practice Script

If you want a quicker practice session, here's a condensed version:

"May it please the Court. I represent the petitioner in this case.

The regulation at issue violates the First Amendment because it restricts core political speech. The documentary film in question addresses matters of public concern and should receive full First Amendment protection.

The government claims this regulation prevents corruption, but independent expenditures like this film do not create the same risk of corruption as direct contributions. There is no quid pro quo arrangement here.

Furthermore, the regulation is unconstitutionally vague. It fails to provide clear guidance about what speech is prohibited, creating a chilling effect on protected speech.

For these reasons, the Court should strike down this regulation. Thank you."

## Creating Your Own Script

When creating your own argument script:

1. **Start with an introduction** - Identify yourself and preview your main points
2. **Structure your argument** - Use numbered points or clear transitions
3. **Include legal principles** - Reference cases, constitutional provisions, or legal standards
4. **Address counterarguments** - Anticipate and respond to opposing arguments
5. **End with a conclusion** - Summarize your key points and request relief

Good luck with your practice!
